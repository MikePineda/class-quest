Welcome back, everyone. Last week we spent most of our time on the mechanics of fitting a model: how a loss function scores a set of predictions, how gradient descent nudges the parameters to lower that score, and why the learning rate matters so much. By the end of the session most of you had a linear model and a small neural network training on the housing data, and the loss curves were going down nicely. This week we are going to ask a question that sounds almost insulting after all that effort: so what? A falling loss curve tells you the model is getting better at something, but we have not yet been careful about what that something is. Today's session is about the gap between fitting and learning, and it is arguably the most important idea in the whole unit. If you take one thing away from the first half of this course, it should be the material we cover in the next hour. We will define training data properly, introduce generalisation, spend a good while on overfitting and how to spot it, then look at validation splits and regularisation as the two standard responses. There is a short exercise at the end.

Let me start with a scenario, because the abstract version of this idea never lands until you have seen it go wrong. Suppose you are building a model to flag fraudulent transactions for a payment company. You are handed a spreadsheet with two hundred thousand rows, each one a past transaction, and a label column that says whether it turned out to be fraud. You build a model, train it on those rows, and it scores 99.4 percent accuracy. You present that number to the team, they are delighted, and the model goes live on Monday morning. By Wednesday it has flagged almost nothing, and the fraud team is asking what happened. Nothing in your training run was wrong. The model did exactly what you asked it to do, which was to reproduce the labels on the rows you gave it. The problem is that nobody ever asked it to do anything else. The transactions that arrive on Monday are not the rows in the spreadsheet. They are new. And the only thing that matters to the company is how the model behaves on the new ones. Keep this scenario in mind, because every definition today is an attempt to make that distinction precise.

So let us be precise about the data. The training set is the portion of the data the model sees during fitting. When we say a model is trained, we mean that its parameters were adjusted, usually by minimising a loss, using exactly these examples and no others. Every number the optimiser saw, every gradient it computed, came from this set. That sounds obvious, but it has a consequence people miss. Any score you compute on the training set is a score on examples the model has already been shown, and possibly shown hundreds of times across epochs. It is a measure of how well the model can reproduce what it was given. I want you to think of training accuracy as a memory test rather than an understanding test. A student who has the answer sheet in front of them during the exam will score well, and that score tells you nothing about whether they could pass a different paper. When you see a training accuracy figure in a report, your first reaction should be to ask what the model scored on data it did not see. If nobody can tell you, the number is close to meaningless.

This brings us to the word that will do most of the work today. Generalisation is the ability to perform well on previously unseen inputs. A model that generalises has picked up on the regularities in the training data that also hold in the world the training data came from. A model that does not generalise has picked up on something that was true of those particular rows and nothing else. Note that generalisation is not a property you can read off the training process. It is defined entirely in terms of data the model has not seen, so you have to go and get some of that data to measure it. I also want to head off a common belief at this point. Students often assume that more data automatically fixes generalisation, and that any model will generalise if you give it enough examples. More data usually helps, and we will see why, but it is one factor among several. A model with enough flexibility to memorise the training set will happily memorise a larger training set too. Model capacity and the constraints you place on it matter independently of how many rows you have. Generalisation is the goal, and training accuracy is at best a loose proxy for it.

Here is an analogy that I find sticks. Imagine two students preparing for an exam using a collection of past papers. The first student works through the papers, notices that certain kinds of question keep appearing, figures out the methods behind them, and practises those methods. The second student memorises the answer to every question on every past paper, word for word, including the specific numbers. Now give both of them one of the past papers as a test. The second student scores a perfect mark, because every question is one they have memorised. The first student scores well but not perfectly, because they are solving rather than recalling. Now give them a brand new paper written by the same examiner. The first student does about as well as before. The second student is lost, because nothing on the new paper matches anything they stored. Both students had the same training data. The difference is in what each one extracted from it. When we talk about a model fitting the data, we have to ask which of these two students it is behaving like, and training accuracy alone cannot tell us.

We now have the vocabulary to name the failure. Overfitting occurs when a model fits noise in the training data rather than the underlying signal. Every real dataset is a mixture of the two. The signal is the relationship that would hold if you collected the data again tomorrow. The noise is everything that happened to be true this time: measurement error, the particular sample of customers you drew, a sensor that drifted for an afternoon, a labeller who was tired on Friday. A model with enough capacity cannot tell the difference between the two. It just sees numbers and a loss it is supposed to reduce, and fitting the noise reduces the loss on the training set just as effectively as fitting the signal. That is why overfitting is a capacity problem rather than a data quality problem. People hear the word noise and assume the dataset must have been dirty. It need not have been. A perfectly clean dataset still contains sampling variation, and a sufficiently flexible model will fit that variation as if it were law. You can overfit clean data just as easily as messy data, and usually more quietly.

How do you spot it? The characteristic signature is a widening gap between training and validation error. If you plot both errors against training time, or against model complexity, you will see the training error fall steadily, because the model is always getting better at reproducing the examples it sees. The validation error, measured on data held back from training, falls at first too, while the model is learning the signal. Then at some point it flattens and turns upward, while training error keeps dropping. That divergence is overfitting in progress. The model has run out of signal to learn and has started fitting noise, which helps on the training set and hurts everywhere else. I want to stress that nothing in the training error itself gives this away. A training score of 99 percent looks exactly like progress, and if that is the only number on your dashboard you will ship a model that fails on Monday. The warning sign is only visible when you have a second curve to compare against. A near-perfect training score with no validation curve next to it is not a result. It is an unanswered question.

It is worth pausing on the opposite failure so that you do not come away thinking that simpler is always better. Underfitting is what happens when the model does not have enough capacity, or has not trained long enough, to capture even the signal. Both training and validation error stay high, and they stay close together, because the model is equally bad on everything. A straight line fitted to data that clearly curves is the textbook example. The two failures sit at opposite ends of a single axis, which we usually call model complexity or capacity. Move along that axis from simple to complex and training error falls the whole way. Validation error falls, reaches a minimum, and rises again. The sweet spot is the minimum of the validation curve, and almost everything we do in practice is an attempt to land near it. This picture is sometimes described as the bias and variance trade-off, and you will meet the formal version of it later in the course. For now the important thing is the shape of the two curves, and the fact that only one of them tells you where you are.

So the practical question is how to get that second curve. The answer is to hold some data back. Before you train anything, you split the dataset, and you set aside a portion that the model will never be fitted on. A held-out validation set provides an unbiased estimate of performance on unseen data. Because the model has never seen those rows, its performance on them is a genuine preview of how it will behave on whatever arrives next week, under the assumption that next week's data looks like this week's. The usual starting point is something like eighty percent for training and twenty percent for validation, but the exact ratio matters less than the discipline of keeping the two apart. A few practical points. The split should be random unless there is a reason for it not to be, such as time-ordered data where you should validate on the most recent period. The split should be made once and recorded, so that every experiment is compared on the same held-out rows. And any preprocessing that learns something from the data, such as scaling or vocabulary building, should be fitted on the training portion only.

Many of you will have heard of a test set as well as a validation set, and the distinction is not pedantic. The validation set is the data you use to make decisions: which model to pick, how many layers, how long to train. The test set is data you touch exactly once, at the very end, to report a final number. Why the extra layer? Because a dataset you make decisions against slowly stops being unseen. Every time you look at the validation score and change something in response, a little information about those rows leaks into your choices. Do that forty times while tuning and your model has been shaped, indirectly, by the validation set, even though no gradient ever touched it. The score is now optimistic. This is the trap of tuning against the test set. It does not feel like training, so the test set still seems untouched, but the leak is real and it compounds with every decision. The remedy is the third split: tune against validation, and reserve a test set that stays sealed until you are done. Cross-validation is a more data-efficient variant of the same idea, and we will cover it in the lab.

A validation split lets you see overfitting. The second tool today lets you do something about it. Regularisation penalises model complexity, biasing the fit toward simpler hypotheses. The idea is to change what the optimiser is minimising. Instead of minimising the training loss alone, you minimise the training loss plus a penalty term that grows as the model becomes more elaborate. For a linear model that might mean the sum of the squared weights. The optimiser now has to justify every bit of complexity by a corresponding reduction in loss, and fitting noise rarely pays for itself under that rule, because noise is by definition not structured enough to be worth a large weight. The result is a model that is pulled toward smoother, simpler explanations of the data. You can think of regularisation as a form of scepticism built into the training procedure. The model is allowed to believe the data, but it has to be convinced, and the strength of the penalty controls how much convincing it takes.

There are several common forms, and you should be able to recognise them by name. L2 regularisation, also called ridge or weight decay, adds the sum of squared weights to the loss and shrinks all of them toward zero. L1 regularisation, or lasso, adds the sum of absolute weights and tends to push many of them to exactly zero, which makes it useful for feature selection. Early stopping is regularisation in disguise: you monitor the validation error during training and stop when it starts to rise, which prevents the model from entering the region where it is fitting noise. Dropout, which you will meet when we get to deep networks, randomly switches off units during training so that no single pathway can memorise a particular example. These look like different techniques, and their mechanics are different, but they share the same purpose. Each one restricts how freely the model can fit the training set, in the expectation that the restriction costs a little on data it has seen and buys a lot on data it has not.

That last sentence contains the trade-off, and I want to make it explicit because students consistently get it wrong. Regularisation is not a free improvement. It deliberately raises the training error. A regularised model fits the training set worse than an unregularised one, by construction, because you have forbidden it from using some of its capacity. The bet is that the training error you give up was being spent on noise, so losing it costs you nothing on new data and may gain you a great deal. That bet can be made too aggressively. Turn the penalty up high enough and the model cannot fit the signal either, and you are back in underfitting territory with both errors high. So the strength of the regularisation is itself a setting you have to choose, and the way you choose it is, once again, by looking at validation error. Try a range of values, pick the one whose validation score is best, and do not be alarmed when the training score at that setting is lower than it was before. That drop is the technique working as intended.

Let me pull this together. A model's performance on the examples it was fitted on tells you what it memorised. Generalisation, which is performance on unseen examples, is the actual goal, and you cannot measure it without holding data back. Overfitting is what happens when capacity is spent on noise, and the only reliable way to see it is a gap opening between training and validation curves. Validation splits are how you see the gap. Regularisation is how you close it, at the price of some training accuracy you were not going to miss. Then you will add L2 regularisation and find the penalty strength that minimises validation error. Write down your training score before and after, and be ready to explain why it went down.
